## 1. Problem Framing

### The 4 Parts of Problem Framing

| Dimension | Question |
|-----------|----------|
| **WHO** | Who is experiencing the problem? Can this user be further specified by persona, motivation? |
| **WHAT** | What are the struggles? What task needs to be accomplished? What pain point needs relief? |
| **WHERE** | What is the context? Physical or digital space? Who else is involved? |
| **WHY** | What value does solving it bring? Why is blockchain the appropriate technology? |

### Problem Statement Qualities
- **Brief** — Condensed to a few sentences so others quickly understand scope
- **Factual & Unbiased** — Look at information with an open, honest mind
- **Rooted** — Focus on root cause, not symptoms
- **Positive & Negative** — Capture opportunities, not only problems

### Solution-Driven Thinking (Anti-Pattern)
Starting with the solution leads to:
- Searching for a problem that justifies your idea
- Artificial use cases
- Unnecessary blockchain usage
- Features nobody asked for
- Products only builders understand

## 2. Defining User Segments & Mental Models

### Why Define Target Users?
1. Turns vague problems into solvable ones
2. Aligns the team around the same user
3. Enables better prioritization & user-centric approach
4. Improves product-market fit
5. Removes bias preventing solution-first thinking
6. Sharpens design and UX decisions

### Three Interdependent Aspects
- **Segments** define who you are building for
- **Mental models** define how they think
- **Personas** make it usable for product decisions

### Segment Dimensions
1. **Goals** — What are they trying to achieve?
2. **Fears** — What are they afraid of?
3. **Knowledge** — What do they already understand?
4. **Behavior** — How do they currently solve this?

### Persona Influence on Product
1. MVP Feature Scope & Prioritization
2. Complexity Level
3. Trust & Safety Design
4. Language & Vocabulary
5. Overall User Experience
6. Product Positioning & Messaging
7. Growth Strategy

## 3. Competitors Research & Market Fit

### Three Types of Competitors
1. **Direct competitors** — Same problem, same customer, similar product
2. **Different solution** — Same problem, same customer, different approach
3. **Different customer** — Same problem, similar solution, different industry/vertical

### Why Understand Competitors?
- Identify your idea's weaknesses, strengths, opportunities
- Learn what worked or failed in the past
- Find your unique value proposition

### Market Fit
Many projects are technically impressive but:
- Solve edge-case problems
- Serve crypto-native users only
- Compete with 10 identical Web3 apps
- Depend on incentives to attract users

### Market Fit Questions
- Who has this problem today?
- Is this a top-3 pain in their life?
- How are they solving it now?
- Why would they switch to my solution?

## 4. Product Direction & Value Proposition

### Product Strategy = 3 Components

| Component | Definition |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Core Purpose** | Why does this product deserve to exist? |
| **Product Direction** | A specific user + use case + outcome |
| **Value Proposition** | Why someone would choose you over alternatives |

### Value Proposition Questions
- Why is this better than what they use today?
- Why is this better than other similar Web3 solutions?
- What is my unique or differential value proposition?

> "If the first thing you explain is the tech, you're building features. If the first thing you explain is the user problem, you're building a product."

## 5. User-Centric Approach for Web3

### Common Web3 Builder Assumptions (Wrong)
- "All users care about decentralization"
- "All users understand wallets"
- "All users want to manage private keys"

### What Users Actually Care About
- Solving their problem
- Feeling safe & not losing money
- Having a small learning curve
- Not getting overwhelmed

### 4 Principles for User-Centric Web3 Products

#### #1 Abstract Complexity
- Automate/hide as many steps as possible
- Remove unnecessary decisions
- Hide technical language
- Prioritize user habits & learned behaviors

**UX Best Practices:**
- Account Abstraction
- Biometric logins
- Social Recovery
- Seedless wallets
- Meta TXs
- Human-Readable Tx Signing

#### #2 Design for Familiarity
- Map blockchain actions to real-world concepts (pay, own, transfer, confirm)
- Preserve established digital behaviors (bank transfers, checkout flows, login patterns)
- Introduce Web3 concepts progressively, not all at once
- Innovate in technology & infrastructure — not in basic behavior

**UX Best Practices:**
- Familiar confirmation patterns
- Clear transaction states
- Safe defaults to reduce decision fatigue
- Highlight irreversible actions visually
- Consistent and familiar terminology

#### #3 Design for Trust
- Clear risk warnings & irreversible actions
- Predictable & simple costs (no surprise fees)
- Error recovery & prevention patterns
- Transaction simulation features
- Human-readable contract actions
- Scam protection & Security Audit validation
- Show status & confirm success in simple language

**UX Best Practices:**
- Audit Badges
- Last Verified Stamp
- Contract Verification
- On-chain Reputation
- Error Prevention & Recovery
- Progressive information disclosure

#### #4 Validate with Users
- Every feature is based on a hypothesis
- Every UX decision is an assumption about user behavior
- Every product idea assumes a real need exists
- Every token model assumes users care about incentives
- Every interface design assumes users understand the UX/UI flow

**UX Best Practices:**
- User interviews
- Task-based usability testing
- Wait-list sign up
- A/B testing

## Key Takeaways

> "Problem framing is not merely about identifying a problem... but about understanding its context, implications, value, viability and potential for innovative solutions."

> "Every product decision is a user decision in disguise. If you don't define the user, the product defines itself or by assumptions — and usually becomes too complex."

> "You're not just competing with other similar platforms... You're also competing with current habits and solutions to similar needs."

> "Clarity of purpose drives focus. Focus creates differentiation. Differentiation creates adoption."

> "Usability is a key competitive advantage — not decentralization alone. Adoption is driven by clarity, not just technology."

## Closing Thoughts

- These steps are interconnected and necessary for developing solid product ideas
- They prevent wasting time, effort, and resources at early product stages
- They help avoid building ideas, features, or products without real impact or adoption
- A structured approach enables small, controlled iterations instead of starting over if product fails
- This allows continuous improvement until the product achieves traction
